Forewarn

Mathare, Nairobi. The night of Tuesday 23 April 2024.

“Maji! Maji!”Water! Water!

People woke in the middle of the night to their neighbours shouting. The river that runs through the valley was already rising into their homes.

That shout was the warning. For most people in Africa, it still is.

The water came at night. The rain had been coming for a month.

In the 30 days before that night, Nairobi had more than twice its normal rain. The ground was full. New rain had nowhere to go but the river.

From 1991 to 2020, only one year was wetter at that time of year. The signs were in the data for weeks. They did not reach the people in the valley.

A warning for every street, not only every country.

Forecasts and warnings exist. Most go to governments and cover whole regions. Forewarn starts at the other end: your door.

Days ahead, in plain words. It reads 51 runs of the world’s leading forecast for your spot and tells you the chance, like “about 1 in 4”.

It knows how wet the ground already is. The month before matters as much as the day. On a farm, the same reading shows a season running dry.

See it on your own street. In Lagos and Nairobi, watch the last flood come back street by street.

Ask it anything. An agent built on Meta’s Muse Spark answers, and it never makes up a number.

What it cannot do yet

  • It will miss some floods. Forecasts can under-call a single violent storm. We test Forewarn on 36 past African floods and show the misses next to the hits.
  • The street-level 3D covers Lagos and Nairobi for now. The forecast and the rain reading cover all of Africa.
  • It does not replace your government’s warnings. If they tell you to leave, leave.
